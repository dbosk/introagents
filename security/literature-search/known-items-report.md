## Per item: what the lecture may say

1. **Riemann hypothesis (Clay problem description, Bombieri).** The Clay Mathematics Institute's description states the hypothesis as "The nontrivial zeros of ζ(s) have real part equal to 1/2", links the zeta function to the primes through Riemann's analytic formula for the number of primes (expressed through the zeros), and says "The failure of the Riemann hypothesis would create havoc in the distribution of prime numbers." (`Bombieri2000Riemann`.) The prime number theorem is **not stated** there (it mentions Gauss's approximation of π(x) by Li(x) and the equivalence of RH with π(x) = Li(x) + O(√x log x)); for it use Tao's open lecture notes: π(x) ∼ x/log x, with li(x) the more accurate form, and the theorem is equivalent to ζ having no zeros on the line Re s = 1 (`Tao2014Notes`). Suggested wording: "π(x) ~ x/ln x, more accurately Li(x)".
2. **Quanta, 19 Oct 2026.** Quasi-RH only asks that all nontrivial zeros lie in a strip narrower than the known 0 < Re s < 1; OpenAI announced two proofs, 1/8 to 7/8 (199 pages, Lean-verified) and a weaker 1/12 to 11/12 (49 pages, not formally verified at the time). ## Definitions for a first-course business student

Plain wording is mine; each is followed by the verbatim source sentence it rests on.

* **Agent** (`Willison2025Agents`, `Anthropic2024Agents`). In plain words: an AI agent is a language model that is allowed to *do* things, not only write text: it calls tools (search, run code, send a request), looks at what came back, and decides the next step itself, round after round, until the goal is reached. Simon Willison, 18 Sep 2025: "An LLM agent runs tools in a loop to achieve a goal." Anthropic, 19 Dec 2024: "Agents, on the other hand, are systems where LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage, maintaining control over how they accomplish tasks." (contrasted with "Workflows are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths."). Both sources say the term has no single accepted definition (Willison: the term "defies attempts to produce a single universally accepted definition"; Anthropic: "'Agent' can be defined in several ways"), so say "one common definition".
* **Token and per-token billing** (`OpenAI2026Tokens`, `Anthropic2026Pricing`, `Anthropic2026Glossary`). In plain words: a token is the chunk of text a model reads and writes, often a short word or a piece of a longer one, roughly three-quarters of an English word; hosted models are billed by counting the tokens you send in (input) and the tokens the model writes back (output), with a separate price for each. OpenAI: "Tokens are the units that OpenAI models use to process text. A token can represent a character, part of a word, a whole word, or punctuation."; "1 token is approximately 4 characters."; "100 tokens are approximately 75 words."; input tokens are "Tokens supplied to the model in a request." and output tokens "Tokens generated by the model."; "For token-based API pricing, the rate depends on the model and token category. Input, cached input, and output tokens can have different prices." Anthropic's pricing page lists per-million-token prices in columns "Input" and "Output" (on 27 Nov 2026 "$8 / MTok" and "$40 / MTok" for the first model listed); it also says "These token counts are added to your normal input and output tokens to calculate the total cost of a request." Prices change and the page is undated: cite the structure, or the access date. Anthropic's glossary warns that the characters-per-token ratio depends on model and language ("For Claude, the number of characters a token represents depends on the model and the language."), so the 4-characters figure is OpenAI's rule of thumb, not a constant.
* **System prompt** (`OWASP2025LLM`, `OpenAI2026Text`). In plain words: the standing instructions the company that builds an AI application writes for the model (role, tone, rules), which the user normally does not see and which come before and above the user's own messages. OWASP (2025 edition, LLM07): "System prompts are designed to guide the model's output based on the requirements of the application, but may inadvertently contain secrets." OpenAI's guide describes the same mechanism under another name: "The instructions parameter gives the model high-level instructions on how it should behave while generating a response, including tone, goals, and examples of correct responses. These instructions take priority over user messages in input." The word "system prompt" itself does not occur in the OpenAI passages found; equating the two is my reading. Lecture-relevant consequence, from OWASP: "the system prompt should not be considered a secret, nor should it be used as a security control." (The 2026 edition renames this entry "Hidden Context Exposure".)

## Disagreements between sources

1. **RH or GRH.** Alpöge's note: RH, then "the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis", expects the Extended RH. Lean FRO/Mathlib: "the Riemann Hypothesis" only. Sutherland, Rubinstein, the Deicyde and Sernau repositories: RH and GRH **for Dirichlet L-functions**. Lee (Conversation): "the broader claim known as the generalised Riemann hypothesis". Sernau's repository description says "Generalized Riemann Hypothesis" while its README restricts to Dirichlet L-functions. Suggested lecture wording: "RH, and GRH for Dirichlet L-functions, in Lean; Alpöge's note claims more".
2. **Lean statement's authors.** The post's byline was edited four times between 21 and 23 Nov ("Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative" versus "Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative"; an earlier text said "part of the Lean and Mathlib communities have rallied"). The live text (27 Nov): "Joint statement by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative." The Conversation says "the Lean Focused Research Organization and the MathLib community".
3. **"250,000+ pages".** In Alpöge's footnote 2 and repeated by the Conversation (23 Nov); Quanta (24 Nov update): he now says it was a joke.
4. **When Alpöge got the result.** Note: "late Sunday night" (15 Nov), with a timeline whose last line is "11/16/26: GRH"; Quanta: "Monday November 16". Minor; avoid the date.
5. **Litt's quotation.** The Conversation ends point (2) at "No human understands it yet."; Litt continues with "There doesn't seem to be a meaningful obstruction to humans understanding it, though ...". Litt also says (22 Nov) that the competing claims by humans seem not credible; Baez (24 Nov) reports an anonymous source saying the AI-reported problems with Masullo's proof are "readily handled".
6. **OpenAI counts.** Quanta (19 Oct) "377 results ... announced on October 6" and "722 OpenAI papers released on October 6"; Buzzard "722 mathematics papers, around 45% of which came with some Lean code" but "290 OpenAI theorems which come with Lean code" (about 40%).
7. **Quasi-RH versions.** Quanta: 1/8 to 7/8 (199 pages, Lean-verified) and 1/12 to 11/12 (49 pages, not formally verified); experts concentrate on the shorter paper. A search-engine summary said accounts differ on which version has a Lean proof; Quanta's text is the only account read in full.
