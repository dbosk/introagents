\emph{Overview}
These notes ask one question of everything a language model produces: who
checks?
They open with what the reader already does with AI and expects of it,
and test those expectations against a current case, the proofs of the
Riemann hypothesis announced in the autumn of 2026.
They then turn to security, where the same question decides what an
attacker can do: first for a company that builds a language model into a
product, tried out against a support chatbot that is vulnerable by
design, and then for the reader's own use of AI agents.

After this lecture, the student should be able to:

\begin{restatable}{lo}{SecLOCheck}\label{SecLOCheck}%
  explain why handing a task to an AI still takes someone who can say
  exactly what is wanted and who can check the result independently of
  the AI that produced it.
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{SecLOEvidence}\label{SecLOEvidence}%
  describe what studies have found about who gains from AI assistance,
  and name what the result depends on.
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{SecLOInjection}\label{SecLOInjection}%
  explain prompt injection: why any text that a language model reads can
  work as an instruction, whoever wrote it.
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{SecLOProduct}\label{SecLOProduct}%
  for a language model built into a product, identify what an outsider
  can read out, do and spend through it, and say where the defence has
  to be placed.
\end{restatable}
\begin{restatable}{lo}{SecLOAgents}\label{SecLOAgents}%
  analyse their own use of an AI agent by what it can read, who can
  write what it reads and what it can do, and choose measures that take
  one of the three away.
\end{restatable}

\emph{Prerequisites}
The student should have written and run small programs in Python and
have tried a chatbot such as ChatGPT, Copilot or Claude.
No mathematics beyond upper secondary school and no earlier course in
security is assumed.
